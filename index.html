Give your characters a voice

Imagine a story where nobody speaks. Conversations bring stories to life by showing readers who characters are, what they feel, and how they relate. Dialogue helps develop personalities, reveal information, and move the story forward.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify the functions of dialogue in revealing personality, emotions, and plot.
  • Apply correct punctuation, capitalization, and paragraph formatting, with varied tags and action beats.
  • Compose an original dialogue scene of 60–100 words with distinct voices that moves a story forward (Browne & King, 2004).

What is dialogue?

Dialogue is the written conversation between two or more characters in a story, play, or other literary work.

“Are you ready for the competition?” Mia asked. “I think so, but I’m still nervous,” Jake replied.

Four purposes of dialogue

A. Reveals personality
How a character speaks shows attitude and feelings.
B. Moves the story forward
Conversations introduce information, problems, and events.
C. Expresses emotions and relationships
Shows how characters feel about one another.
D. Makes stories engaging
Breaks up description and lets readers “see” the conversation.

Building blocks

Speaker – the character saying the lines.

Dialogue tag – words that tell who is speaking: saidaskedrepliedwhisperedshoutedexplainedansweredexclaimed

Action beat – a description of movement, gesture, or expression while speaking.

Tag: “Is that the blue one?” Mark asked. Beat: Mark looked around the classroom. “Is that the blue one?”

Punctuation & formatting rules

  1. Use double quotation marks around the exact words spoken.
  2. Use a comma before the closing quote when a dialogue tag follows.
  3. Put question marks and exclamation points inside the quotation marks.
  4. Start a new paragraph every time a different character speaks.

5 ways to make dialogue effective

  • Make it sound natural and believable.
  • Give each character a unique voice.
  • Keep every line meaningful – it should have a purpose.
  • Avoid unnecessary, useless conversation.
  • Balance dialogue with actions and descriptions.

Models and examples

Model 1 – Natural, realistic dialogue

“Did you finish the reading?” Mia asked. “Barely. I fell asleep halfway through,” Jake admitted.

Why it works: contractions and everyday words sound like real speech, and Jake’s honest answer shows he is tired and relaxed.

Model 2 – Dialogue tags and action beats

Mia tapped her pen on the desk. “Are we really doing this today?”

Why it works: the action beat shows impatience without saying “she was impatient,” and it makes the speaker clear without a tag.

Model 3 – Punctuation and formatting

“We’re late,” Jake said. “Then run!” Mia shouted.

Why it works: a comma ends the first spoken line before the tag, the “!” stays inside the quotation marks, and each speaker gets a new line.

Model 4 – Dialogue, action, and story development

Daniel stared at the empty doghouse. “Max?” he whispered. “The gate’s open,” Sophie said, already running.

Why it works: the lines introduce a problem (a missing dog) and push the story into its next action.

Crafting dynamic dialogue

Write an original scene of 60–100 words with two people in a tense or uncomfortable situation. Do not tell readers how characters feel – let dialogue, hidden meanings, and small actions show it.

  • New indented paragraph each time a different character speaks.
  • All end punctuation goes inside the quotation marks.
  • Vary tags sparingly (muttered, interrupted, demanded) and balance them with action beats.
  • 4–6 spoken lines.
